# Unique-SAT, quantum SAT baselines and complexity-model corrections

This source review supplements the historical [literature review](../literature_review.md). Each external theorem below remains **cited, not Lean-verified**. Its cost model must be retained when comparing it to RCP-QI.

## Valiant–Vazirani: uniqueness does not dissolve SAT hardness

[Valiant and Vazirani, *NP is as easy as detecting unique solutions* (Theoretical Computer Science 47, 1986)](https://ics.uci.edu/~vazirani/ValVaz.pdf) give a randomized polynomial-time reduction from SAT to the zero-or-one-solution **promise problem**. They isolate solutions by randomly selected GF(2) linear hash constraints; their original proof obtains inverse-polynomial isolation probability, which can be amplified by polynomially many independent trials. An unsatisfiable input stays unsatisfiable. On satisfiable inputs, one trial has a controlled probability of yielding exactly one solution. Their formal oracle formulation permits arbitrary behavior outside the zero/one promise. Exactly-one-solution *search* is a different promise from deciding zero versus one, and witness recovery uses an appropriate self-reduction or a search version, with its own calls and promise handling. The theorem does not prove that every particular unique formula is hard or that an arbitrary promise-preserving branch query is cheap.

Consequently the V1–V7 unique-marker model is a useful controlled search setting, but uniqueness alone does not make explicit SAT easy. The algebraic fact `G(A) XOR G(B)=1` for disjoint exhaustive halves holds only when a unique witness exists; it does not implement `G(A)` or certify uniqueness. A zero-solution formula has neither half marked; multiple solutions may mark both. Valiant–Vazirani is **randomized** and cannot be silently substituted for a deterministic, always-correct promise reduction.

## Quantum 3-SAT comparisons beyond full-space Grover

[Ambainis, *Quantum Search Algorithms* (SIGACT News 2004; arXiv version 2005), §3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0504012) describes coherent amplification of a Schöning/Rolf-style randomized local-search trial. The cited classical success probability is at least `1/(1.329…^n)` per polynomial-time trial; amplitude amplification takes `O*(√(1.329…^n))=O*(1.153…^n)` trials, with bounded error and suppressed polynomial factors. This requires a reversible implementation of the trial, its inverse and success verification. It is an **explicit-input time** statement under that ideal circuit-cost accounting, not merely a black-box phase-query count, and it is not a fault-tolerant hardware estimate. The underlying Schöning walk starts from a random assignment and repeatedly repairs an unsatisfied clause; it is structurally informed even though its asymptotic bound is exponential.

Full-space unique-marker Grover costs `Θ(2^(n/2))≈Θ(1.414^n)` predicate queries before clause-oracle compilation. [Montanaro's quantum backtracking](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.02374) depends on the classical tree size and coherent branch/check operations; [Rennela et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07040) analyze hybrid divide-and-conquer and PPSZ-related quantum speedups under stated tree/instance conditions. [Brehm and Weggemans' structure-aware resource study](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2026-01-20-1975/) compares fault-tolerant backtracking and Grover estimates with modern SAT solving. All are relevant baselines. Later classical [PPSZ analyses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.11071) and a [2026 preprint refinement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10697) improve randomized 3-SAT upper bounds, but simply taking the square root of a reported classical base does **not** by itself certify a coherent implementation with the same asymptotic time. This review does not assert a current record quantum 3-SAT exponent; no comprehensive proof-quality record comparison or fault-tolerant implementation is provided. For explicit instances, matched modern CDCL remains indispensable.

The [new partial-search control](PARTIAL_SEARCH_RESULTS.md) is a **generic equality-oracle block-label** experiment, not a 3-SAT solver. Its constant-factor query saving cannot replace these structure-aware SAT comparators.

## QSETH is a conjecture, with distinct formulations

[Aaronson, Chia, Lin, Wang and Zhang (CCC 2020)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.01973) define a quantum fine-grained QSETH form: for every `ε>0`, some clause width `k` resists `O(2^((1/2−ε)n))`-time quantum algorithms for `k`-SAT (up to the paper's polynomial conventions). [Buhrman, Patro and Speelman (2019 preprint; STACS 2021 framework)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.05686) formulate a basic CNF-SAT/representation-class `γ` conjecture and extended **property-specific** conjectures (e.g. properties of the satisfaction truth table), with compression-obliviousness conditions. Neither is an unconditional lower bound. A fixed 3-SAT exponent below `1.414^n` does not refute a statement quantified over sufficiently large `k`. These conjectures concern explicit-input quantum **time** and cannot be inferred from a phase-oracle query bound.

## Black-box and relativization limits

[Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9701001) establish oracle-model limitations on unstructured search and exhibit relativized separations. Such results constrain algorithms whose only access to the marked function is the charged oracle. They do not rule out a structured explicit-CNF algorithm that inspects clauses and exploits special promises. Saying a successful approach "must be non-relativizing" without specifying a proof technique is too broad: oracle separation concerns which conclusions relativizing arguments can establish, while a particular algorithm can exploit non-black-box input structure without itself being a proof that `NP⊄BQP`. No general quantum SAT gate lower bound follows.

## Backdoor sets are established structural-promise prior art

[Williams, Gomes and Selman (IJCAI 2003)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/selman/papers/pdf/03.ijcai.backdoors.pdf) introduce small variable sets whose assignment moves a SAT/CSP instance into a tractable *sub-solver* class. A **weak** backdoor has some assignment that lets the sub-solver find a satisfying residual; a **strong** backdoor works for every assignment, allowing `2^b` residual checks for `b` backdoor variables and UNSAT certification when every branch is rejected. Given a proposed set and a tractable syntactic base class, strong membership can be checked by enumerating its `2^b` assignments; a weak witness includes a branch and a checked satisfying residual. Discovery of a small set is a separate optimization/search problem whose complexity depends on the base class. [Gaspers and Szeider](https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.6387) survey parameterized detection results; their [acyclic-SAT study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.6384) gives both positive and hardness results for specific base classes. No universal cheap discovery claim follows.

This substantially overlaps V7.1's "cheaply certified structural promise" vocabulary. A supplied small backdoor is useful classically, and a quantum use must charge discovery, coherent residual evaluation, state preparation and comparison with `2^b` classical branching or quantum amplification/backtracking. The RCP-QI repository does not claim origin of the backdoor framework.

## The one-iteration `4/N` bias and different information tasks

For one marker, V1 derives `P(correct half)=1/2+δ`, `δ=4/N−8/N²=Θ(1/N)`. If the target is in the opposite half, a fixed-half measurement has probability `1/2−δ`. Distinguishing these Bernoulli distributions, or estimating the bias from independent executions of the one-iteration circuit, needs `Θ(δ^-2)=Θ(N²)` samples for constant confidence; each run prepares a new state and uses one phase query. This is a statement about that **fixed measurement protocol**, not all quantum procedures.

Coherent [amplitude estimation](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0005055) can estimate an additive probability difference at cost `O(1/δ)=O(N)` uses of a controlled preparation/reflection construction, provided its unitary and inverse are available; those calls include the underlying phase oracle, state preparation/inverse and half-marking reflections. It improves over independent sampling but is still not the best way to locate a generic half. Grover–Radhakrishnan partial search and generic adversary reasoning retain `Θ(√N)` for constant-success half identification. One must not infer an `Ω(N)` lower bound for **all** coherent circuits from the one-iteration bias.

[Abrams and Lloyd (1998)](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9801041) study hypothetical **nonlinear** quantum evolutions; [Aaronson's PostBQP = PP theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0412187) grants **postselection** as an extra computational resource. Neither operation is supplied for free by standard unitary circuits or Qiskit statevectors. Rare-event conditioning has a real success-probability/repetition cost in an ordinary experiment.
